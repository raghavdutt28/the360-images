Gate 3, 2:14 a.m. Someone steps into a restricted zone. A general-purpose LLM can describe the scene in a tidy paragraph. But the control room doesn’t need a paragraph. It needs a tracker around the right object, a label, and an alert, on every frame, from every camera, all night.

General LLMs are built to do everything: write code, translate, summarise contracts. That breadth is paid for in size, speed and precision. On a focussed, repetitive job like detection, a small model trained for that one task usually wins.

Researchers from Roboflow and Carnegie Mellon tested detectors and vision-language models on 100 real-world detection datasets. A fine-tuned YOLO11n, at 2.6 million parameters, averaged 55.3 mAP. Gemini 2.5 Pro, used out of the box, averaged 13.3. Qwen2.5-VL-72B managed 7.4. Trained-for-the-task versus general-purpose: exactly the choice a buyer faces.

Source: Roboflow100-VL benchmark paper ↗︎

The market is moving the same way. Gartner predicts that by 2027, organisations will use small, task-specific AI models at least three times more than general-purpose LLMs, citing falling accuracy on domain tasks and lower compute costs.

Source: Gartner press release, April 2025 ↗︎

Large Language Models like ChatGPT or Claude are huge sized & very expensive.

YOLO11n, a compact object detector, has 2.6 million parameters and processes a frame in about 1.5 milliseconds on an NVIDIA T4 GPU. Qwen2.5-VL, one of the strongest open vision-language models, has 72 billion: nearly 28,000 times more.

Source: Ultralytics YOLO11 benchmarks ↗︎

Cloud video models bill by the token. Google’s Gemini API counts video at 263 tokens per second. One camera for 24 hours is 22.7 million tokens. Fifty cameras is over a billion tokens a day, before anyone has asked a single question, and every frame has to leave your site to get there.

Source: Gemini API token documentation ↗︎

Then there’s the output. An LLM answers in prose, and prose can hedge, drift or sound confident while being wrong. A detector returns a class, a box and a confidence score: structured evidence a supervisor can act on and an auditor can check.
